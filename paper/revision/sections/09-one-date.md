## 9. What to do when the register codes one date

Focal isolation identifies a width only where a source dates the relation and the event from separate fields, and most registers do not; corpus A does not, for 94 percent of its events. A practitioner turning to a single-dated register will therefore find that the measurement returns zero by entailment. Five things can be done instead, in descending order of what they establish, of which the fifth is a condition on the other four.

**Match a second date from outside the register.** The width is identified as soon as any second dating of the same event exists, and it need not come from the same source: the structure is available whenever an external source records when an event was *decided* against when the register records it as *effective*. Join on the event key, keep only events matched in both, report the match rate, and run focal isolation against the externally dated event. An unmatched majority is a reason to report the width for a subpopulation, not to abandon it.

**Audit a sample of events against the primary source.** Draw $k$ events at random, return to the document the register was coded from, and record the date that document supports. This is expensive per event and cheap in total, since even $k = 50$ bounds the share of events whose coded date is displaced, and the supplementary material gives the mapping from that share to the width focal isolation would read. It is also the only procedure here that can detect a systematic displacement in the register as a whole.

**Report the sensitivity of the result to the horizon rather than the horizon.** If the width cannot be measured, the quantity a practitioner actually needs remains available: how much does the conclusion depend on it? Fit at each lead time under the assumption that the horizon is $\beta$, for $\beta$ across a declared range, and report the range of the performance figure rather than one number. A result that survives every plausible $\beta$ needs no horizon estimate, and a result that does not was resting on one. This establishes least about the width of the four, and on a register we could not date twice it is nonetheless the first thing we would report.

**Read the coding rules and declare the channels.** Section 7.6 is the evidence that this works. Reading is not a substitute for measuring a width, but it identifies which relations can carry the event at all, and that is the decision most exposed to being made by a statistic that cannot make it. Then deposit the declaration somewhere that dates it: the reading is the cheap half and the dating is the half that is easy to get wrong.

**Measure on every register that feeds the target relation.** A second date, an audited sample or a declared reading covers only the register it was taken on, and if the corpus contains more than one dating convention the widest one governs. Section 7.3 measures the cost of the narrower reading at a mean of 0.82 to 2.68 periods and a maximum of nine. The check that says whether this matters is cheap and is not a statistic: **count the dating conventions the sources declare.** Then make the range of lead times examined at least as wide as the widest convention, because the instrument censors at the top of its range exactly as the pooled statistics do.

What none of these does is recover a horizon from the target relation's pooled density, for the reason Section 7.2 measures: once homophily is strong enough, the pooled statistic's output is independent of the true width.
