## 2. What this problem is, and three problems it is not

The failure described above resembles several well-studied problems without being any of them, closely enough that an instrument borrowed from the wrong one will return a confident wrong answer.

### 2.1 The horizon is a gap between two clocks

Database practice distinguishes two timestamps on any recorded fact (Snodgrass, 1999; Jensen and Snodgrass, 1999). *Valid time* is the interval over which the fact holds in the modelled world: the years a state was a member of an organisation. *Transaction time* is the interval over which the database itself held it, meaning the date a curator entered it or could have entered it.

A curated register stores valid time. A forecaster needs transaction time, because at origin $t$ it may use only facts available at $t$. Registers are compiled retrospectively, so their transaction time is a single late date for the whole corpus. What a register does record is a dating convention: the rule by which it assigns valid time to an event it learned about later. When that rule dates a membership from the signature of a founding convention rather than from the date an organisation began operating, the valid-time interval opens before the event became knowable, and a relational feature derived from it carries information at origins where no one could have had it. The **leakage horizon** $\Delta^{\star}$ is the width of that opening: the smallest lead time at which the target relation carries no administrative trace of the event. It is a property of a register's coding rules, not of the entities the register describes.

### 2.2 Three neighbours

**It is not a violation of the temporal split.** The canonical treatment of leakage separates data available at prediction time from data used to build features (Kaufman et al., 2012), and the taxonomy of Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) gives temporal leakage as a split that is not chronological. Ours is chronological, and the contamination survives it. The illegitimacy lies not in the analyst's boundary but in the register's dating rule, which places a legitimate feature at an illegitimate time.

**It is not selection on the outcome.** A register compiled after the fact invites a second reading, under which entities enter it *because* of their outcomes: a state appears in a membership register because it joined something. That is sampling on the dependent variable, whose consequences are well understood. Conditioning on an inclusion indicator that is a common effect of several causes opens a non-causal path between them. The phenomenon is Berkson's bias in epidemiology (Berkson, 1946), collider conditioning in the graphical vocabulary (Pearl, 2009; Hernán et al., 2004; Elwert and Winship, 2014), a missing-not-at-random mechanism in the missing-data vocabulary (Rubin, 1976; Little and Rubin, 2019), and selection on the dependent variable in the social sciences (Geddes, 1990; King et al., 1994). Its effects run against intuition: a feature that strongly predicts membership in the wider population looks weak once every entity in the sample is a member, much as success will seem nearly independent of looks and of ability among actors selected for having appeared in at least twenty films. Heckman (1979) gives the classical correction, and Bareinboim and Pearl (2012) the graphical conditions under which a population distribution is recoverable from selected data at all.

The two problems can be told apart by asking where the defect sits. Under selection on the outcome, the sample is drawn non-randomly and the features are measured correctly on the entities drawn. Under retrospective dating, the sample may be a census, and the features are measured correctly and stamped with the wrong time. We insist on the distinction because it decides what evidence can settle the question. Compensating for selection requires assumptions about a population that was never sampled, and those assumptions cannot be tested on the sample. Compensating for retrospective dating requires a fact about the register — which field it dates from, and what its coding rule does when that field is absent — and such a fact is documentary. It is printed in a codebook and can be read before any statistic is computed. In the fortunate case the register codes both dates, so the width is in the data itself; Section 6.3 is that case.

**It is not noise in the label.** The accession dates we forecast are the register's own, and we take them as given. What the horizon measures is a gap between the date the register assigns to the *relation* and the date it assigns to the *event*. Where a register codes those from separate fields the gap is observable per event. Where it codes one date per membership and derives the event from a transition in that same field, a trace statistic returns zero because the register cannot express the quantity, not because the quantity is absent. We mark every headline result with which side of that line it falls on, since reading an entailment as a measurement is what turns a bounded claim into an inflated one.

### 2.3 Where selection does operate here

The design conditions on outcomes in four places, and each affects what the reported horizon is a horizon of. We name them because an unnamed conditioning step is indistinguishable from an unnoticed one.

The **comparison stratum** is selected on the future. At positive lead times "no event" is ambiguous, since a candidate acceding one year from now is a non-event for a three-year horizon and already carries whatever traces the statistic looks for. The *never-event* stratum holds candidates that never accede to that group anywhere in the observation window, and membership of it is decided by outcomes after the origin, so it conditions on a descendant of the label. We declare it the estimand regardless, because the alternative mixes the quantity of interest into the comparison class, and we also compute the full complement so the gap between them stays visible. Every horizon in this paper is therefore relative to a declared stratum, not to the population.

The **risk set** conditions on survival, since a dyad leaves it when it accedes. This is standard discrete-time practice (Allison, 1982) and conditions on the outcome history up to the origin rather than after it, so it opens no collider path. Entities that left a group and rejoined do re-enter, so we condition every trace statistic on first entry and report the corpus's recurrence rate beside any horizon.

The **analysis window** truncates on the left at 1965. The consequence is central rather than incidental: the one stratum in which back-dating is measured rather than entailed consists entirely of organisations founded before 1965, so the pooled statistic cannot be read on those events at any positive lead time without reaching into the coverage break the window exists to exclude. The two instruments cannot both be measurements on the same events in that corpus.

Finally, the **negatives in the second corpus are constructed**, and every contrast we report there depends on how they were drawn (Poursafaei et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2015). Section 6.5 states the sampler and verifies that it does not avoid existing edges.

### 2.4 What is identified

Write $\kappa$ for a contrast taken over the whole target relation. It is a sum of contributions, of which the administrative trace is one:

$$\kappa(\Delta) = \tau(\Delta) + \lambda(\Delta) + \eta(\Delta) + \rho(\Delta) + \varepsilon$$

Here $\tau$ is the administrative trace on the focal group's own record and $\lambda$ the trace of the same event carried by another group's record. The rest are persistent similarity between the candidate and the members, $\eta$; prior membership spells, $\rho$; and sampling noise, $\varepsilon$. Figure 1 draws the five paths. The identification problem is the third term against the first: a candidate denser to the members because the register back-dated its accession, and a candidate denser because it was always similar to them, enter $\kappa$ identically, so no function of the observation graph alone separates $\tau$ from $\eta$.

A reader arriving from the selection-bias literature will expect the conclusion that nothing here is recoverable without information about the process that generated the data. That expectation is correct, and we take it to be the useful thing this paper has to say rather than an objection to it. Our contribution is to make it quantitative and to say what the outside information has to be. Quantitative, because Section 7.2 measures the cost of trying to do without it: as homophily concentrates, the pooled statistic's slope on the true width goes from 1.00 to 0.00, and over a wider range to about minus one. Specific, because exactly two kinds of outside information suffice. One is a second coded date for the same event, which makes the width observable. The other is a documentary reading of what each relation records, made before any statistic is computed, which identifies the relations that *can* carry the event at all. Neither is a modelling assumption about an unobserved population; both are facts obtained by reading a data-collection process's documentation.
