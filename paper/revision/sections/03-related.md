## 3. Related work

**Label leakage.** Features that encode the label are long recognised in applied machine learning. Kaufman et al. (2012) give the canonical treatment, distinguishing leakage in training examples from leakage in feature construction, and Rao et al. (2008) show how a resampling scheme that looks correct can leak through preprocessing. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) survey 294 papers across seventeen fields and give a taxonomy with eight leaves, in which Section 2.2 places the horizon; the consensus reporting standard that followed is REFORMS (Kapoor et al., 2024). What this literature supplies is checklists and taxonomies. It does not supply an estimator that takes a relation and a lead time and returns how far a register's dating reaches, which is the gap this paper works in.

**Temporal validation of graph learning.** Evaluation protocols for graph neural networks repeatedly turn out to decide reported gains. Splitting and tuning choices dominate architectural comparisons on citation benchmarks (Shchur et al., 2018), which motivated benchmarks with explicit temporal splits (Hu et al., 2020) and their extension to temporal graphs (Huang et al., 2023). The same holds for link prediction, where class imbalance and negative sampling decide the metric more than the method does (Yang et al., 2015). Our finding is complementary and sharper in one respect: we exhibit a corpus in which the temporal split is in place and correct, and a leakage diagnostic applied on top of it reports an eight-year contamination that is not there. Poursafaei et al. (2022) show that a large share of the edges in dynamic link prediction benchmarks repeat edges seen earlier, and our recurrence mechanism is the group-membership form of that observation, for which we claim no priority. What we add is that in a *leakage diagnostic* the confound does not merely inflate a score; it manufactures a decaying curve with the shape of a horizon.

**Selection, missing data and recoverability.** Sections 2.2 and 2.3 set out why the horizon is not a selection problem and where the design nevertheless conditions on outcomes. We draw there on the structural treatment of selection bias (Hernán et al., 2004; Elwert and Winship, 2014) and its origin in Berkson (1946), on the missing-data formulation (Rubin, 1976; Little and Rubin, 2019), on the classical correction (Heckman, 1979), and on the graphical conditions for recovering a population distribution from selected data (Bareinboim and Pearl, 2012); Griffith et al. (2020) demonstrate how large the resulting distortions can be in practice. We use this work to say precisely what our problem is not, and to borrow its central lesson: no amount of data repairs a defect in how the data came to exist.

**Measurement validity and provenance.** The paper is a construct validity argument throughout, in the sense of Cronbach and Meehl (1955), who introduced the notion for tests with no criterion to be checked against — the position a leakage diagnostic is in. Jacobs and Wallach (2021) carry that framework into machine learning, separating the unobservable construct from its operationalisation, and our separation of what is measured from what a register's structure entails applies their distinction to one estimand. The parallel pattern of shortcut learning is set out by Geirhos et al. (2020), with its sharpest instances in natural language inference (Gururangan et al., 2018; McCoy et al., 2019); those are instructive about a limit, since a diagnostic set is available when a researcher can write the counterexamples and a leakage horizon has none, which is why our version needs a generator. On the data side, provenance is treated as a first-class artefact (Simmhan et al., 2005; Cheney et al., 2009) and documentation standards converge on recording origin as a condition of release (Gebru et al., 2021; Mitchell et al., 2019; Paullada et al., 2021; Sambasivan et al., 2021), yet reporting where labels came from remains uncommon (Geiger et al., 2020). The temporal half of that literature is the one we lean on most and the one least often cited in machine learning: valid time against transaction time (Snodgrass, 1999; Jensen and Snodgrass, 1999) is exactly what a retrospectively compiled register erases.

**Group prediction and reporting practice.** Hyperedge prediction supplies the task formulation (Benson et al., 2018; Do et al., 2020; Yoon et al., 2020), and the encoders we compare are standard: neighbourhood aggregation (Hamilton et al., 2017), its relation-specific form (Schlichtkrull et al., 2018), and a temporal encoder over the graph history (Rossi et al., 2020). Because a margin between two models is not interpretable without the spread of the seeds behind it, we follow Henderson et al. (2018) and Dodge et al. (2019). The field application is accession to intergovernmental organisations, for which the Correlates of War project supplies a register with documented coding rules (Pevehouse et al., 2020), descended from a version 2 release (Pevehouse et al., 2004) whose documentation states the dating conventions this paper measures. The protein testbed uses CORUM (Steinkamp et al., 2025) and Complex Portal (Meldal et al., 2022) complexes against the STRING network (Szklarczyk et al., 2023); the community assessment of module identification on such networks (Choobdar et al., 2019) is why we treat the curated-register channel as the label source rather than as one predictor among ten.
