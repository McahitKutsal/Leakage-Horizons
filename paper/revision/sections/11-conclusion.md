## 11. Conclusion

Relational features for group membership prediction are usually drawn from the register that supplies the labels, and registers date their contents by conventions that can place an edge before the event it belongs to. Because the contamination sits inside the register, a correct temporal split does not remove it. We formalised its reach as a leakage horizon, built a diagnostic suite to estimate it, and found that the instrument the leakage literature implies does not measure that quantity.

Three measurements establish this. On a generator where the width is set and the target relation's density is held exactly invariant, the pooled statistic's slope on the true width falls from 1.00 to 0.00 as homophily concentrates, while a statistic read on the focal group's own record stays exactly unbiased. On a trade register coding the width itself, the back-dated dyads are 6.8 percent of the relation and the pooled reading is dominated by the other 93.2 percent. On a coalition corpus of 562 groups the pooled reading reports a contrast still at 0.1490 after eight years, indistinguishable there from a relation that cannot record the event, while the group's own record carries nothing past one year.

The reason is not a defect of implementation. A density over the whole target relation cannot distinguish a trace of the event from a persistent similarity between the acceding entity and the members, and no amount of data or thresholding touches that. Identification requires information from outside the graph, and exactly two kinds serve: a second coded date for the same event, or a documentary reading of what each relation records, made before any statistic is computed. Both are facts about a data-collection process, obtained by reading its documentation. The horizon is estimable to the extent that a practitioner is willing to do that reading — a conclusion about where the evidence has to come from, not a counsel of despair.

The practical guidance is specific. Measure back-dating on a group's own record rather than on a pooled density, and on every register feeding the target relation. Where the register codes one date, which is the common case, Section 9 gives five procedures, of which reporting the sensitivity of the result to an assumed horizon is the one we would do first. Condition any trace statistic on first entry and report the recurrence rate beside any horizon. Report a linear probe alongside any non-linear one, and an exclusion contrast at each lead time rather than the level of a decay curve. Stratify any clique-based statistic by group size, and state the level it is read at. Declare the target relation and the anchors from what each source records, before computing anything, and deposit the declaration somewhere that dates it. Put an interval on every magnitude, including the lead time a rule returns. Report both a pooled and a within-group discrimination for any group membership task, since ours differ by about 0.25. And report every parameter-matched architectural margin against its seed spread: four seeds made an encoder significantly worse than its control look like a tie.

The broader point is that a leakage audit is itself a measurement, and a measurement can be wrong in both directions. An instrument that invents an eight-year horizon costs a practitioner eight years of training data. The same instrument, pointed at a register whose back-dated dyads are a small share of a saturated relation, will report nothing where a real contamination sits. Which of the two happens is decided by properties of the corpus — how saturated the target relation is, how much structure the anchors share with it, how often entities recur — that a practitioner can measure before trusting the audit, and that we recommend measuring first.
